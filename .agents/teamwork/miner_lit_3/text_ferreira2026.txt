Abstract
We show that the covariance matrix of any cylindrically symmetric coherent orbital angular
momentum (OAM) eigenmode with quantum number ℓtakes a universal form depending only on
⟨r2⟩, ⟨k2
r⟩, and ℓ, independently of the radial profile, and that this form is identical to the covari-
ance matrix of a twisted Gaussian Schell-model (TGSM) beam. More specifically, both matri-
ces share the same pattern of zero and nonzero entries, with the off-diagonal blocks proportional
to ℓand the TGSM twist parameter u, respectively. This result holds for an arbitrary radial pro-
file and provides direct term-by-term identification of parameters between the two sets of beams.
We work out the correspondence in detail for three important families: Laguerre–Gaussian (LG),
Bessel–Gaussian (BG), and perfect vortex beams (PVBs), and derive the conditions under which
each coherent OAM mode maps onto a physically realizable TGSM beam. Because the covariance
matrix governs second-moment evolution under arbitrary ABCD (symplectic) transformations,
any two beams sharing the same covariance matrix are second-order indistinguishable at every
propagation plane. In particular, the matched TGSM and coherent OAM beams share identical
beam-width evolution, far-field divergence, and M2 beam-quality factor. In particular, the well-
developed TGSM propagation toolbox applies directly to the second-order moment evolution of
the three coherent families. We further show that within each beam family the covariance matrix
uniquely determines the beam parameters. Additional results include cross-family second-moment
equivalence conditions and a proof that the PVB modes span the full space of transverse fields for
any finite ring width, and form an orthonormal (delta-normalized) basis in the narrow-ring limit
w →0.
1. Introduction
The early 1990 s witnessed two major and largely independent developments in the study of structured
optical fields. The first was the renewed recognition that paraxial optical beams may carry quantized
orbital angular momentum (OAM) given by ℓper photon, following the landmark work of Allen et al
[1], which identified the Laguerre–Gaussian (LG) modes as eigenfunctions of the OAM operator Lz.
Closely related families such as Bessel–Gaussian (BG) beams and other vortex-type solutions were shown
to carry similar azimuthal structure [2, 3]. These beams became tools of central importance in classical
optics, with applications in optical manipulation, holography, microscopy, and classical multiplexing in
high-dimensional OAM spaces, and the study of topological defects and singular optics [4–8]. In quan-
tum optics, OAM modes have been used for quantum information encoding in high-dimensional Hilbert
spaces [9], quantum state engineering [10], metrology [11].

The second was the generalization of classical coherence theory to include twisted Gaussian Schell-
model (TGSM) beams, introduced as an extension of the well-known Gaussian Schell-model formulation
of partially coherent fields [12–14]. Classical Gaussian Schell-model beams are defined by a Gaussian
intensity profile and a Gaussian spatial coherence function. TGSM beams extend this formulation by
incorporating a nontrivial twist produced by a phase term coupling the transverse coordinates in the
mutual coherence function. TGSM beams represent the first class of partially coherent fields whose
second-order statistics naturally encode nonzero OAM, even in the absence of a coherent wavefront vor-
tex. They provided a pathway for generalizing concepts such as optical vorticity, phase-space rotation,
and coherence-induced beam shaping to the partially coherent regime [15, 16]. Applications of TGSM
beams include more robust free-space optical propagation in turbulent media [17, 18], sub-Rayleigh
imaging [19], suppression of scintillation [20] and optical trapping [21]. More recently, TGSM beams
have been generated experimentally and their twist phase measured and controlled [22–26]. Their role as
input beams in non-linear optical processes has been studied both theoretically and experimentally [27–
31], where it has been shown that the twist phase is transferred to the correlations of down-converted
photon pairs [27–29, 31]. More recently, the Gaussian–Schell model has also been formulated in the
quantum domain: Dawkins et al introduced a quantum Gaussian–Schell model that directly links
Gaussian coherence theory to the quantum statistical properties of light [32], You et al extended these
ideas to multiphoton systems carrying OAM, isolating the classical and quantum contributions to the
coherence and analyzing correlation properties in OAM space [33], and Toscano et al introduced a quan-
tum Twisted Gaussian Schell state defined in two bosonic modes [34].
Despite their parallel development, TGSM beams and OAM-eigenstate beams originate from con-
ceptually distinct frameworks. TGSM beams arise from second-order coherence theory and are charac-
terized through their mutual coherence function. A central object in the description of partially coher-
ent Gaussian beams (such as TGSMs) is the second-order moment (covariance) matrix, which can be
calculated from their Wigner distribution or from their mutual intensity. For Gaussian beams such as
the TGSM, this matrix completely characterizes the field: its elements encode beam widths, curvatures,
coherence lengths, and correlations between spatial and angular degrees of freedom.
On the other hand, OAM eigenstates, given by
ψ(⃗r) = U (r)eiℓϕ,
(1)
are characterized as fully-coherent solutions of the paraxial wave equation with well-defined OAM pro-
portional to ℓ. The quantum number ℓis also known as the topological charge, and we use these two
terms interchangeably. In general these are not gaussian beams, and thus in principle they cannot be
characterized only by considering their first and second-order moments. For a fully coherent OAM
eigenstate of the form (1), the CM is computed from the intensity distribution |ψ|2 and its phase gra-
dients; it therefore depends on the radial profile U(r) through the moments ⟨r2⟩and ⟨k2
r⟩.
The connection between second-order moments and OAM has been explored in several contexts.
Gao, Wei and Weber showed that the OAM of an arbitrary beam is encoded in the cross-moments
⟨xky⟩−⟨ykx⟩[35], and Serna et al performed a complete second-order moment characterization of
a doughnut-type beam, confirming agreement with LG theory [36]. The second-order properties of
individual cylindrically symmetric beam families have been extensively investigated. In particular, M2-
factor and second-moment width calculations have been reported for LG and BG beams [37–39], while
the second-moment radius of perfect vortex beams (PVBs) has also been studied [40]. Simon and
Mukunda’s original analysis of TGSM beams identified their normal-mode decomposition in terms of
LG functions [28, 41], thus demonstrating that the TGSM is a statistical mixture of LG modes.
The present work operates at a different level, showing that each individual coherent OAM eigen-
state already has a CM with the same structure as a TGSM beam, without any decomposition. By ‘same
structure’ we mean that both matrices share the same pattern of zero and nonzero entries, so that a
direct term-by-term identification of beam parameters is possible. Moreover, the xky and ykx off-diagonal
blocks relevant to angular momentum are proportional to the OAM quantum number ℓand twist phase
u, respectively. We derive explicit parameter identifications for three canonical beam families. Specifically,
this structural equivalence does not appear to have been stated explicitly in the literature, and provides a
direct bridge between the coherent structured-beam and partially coherent communities.
The rest of this paper is organized as follows. In section 2 we review the TGSM covariance matrix
formalism. Section 3 derives the CM of a general cylindrically symmetric OAM eigenstate and identi-
fies it with the TGSM form, then works out the explicit parameter identifications for LG, PVB, and BG
modes, derives the physical realizability conditions, examines cross-family parameter relations, and shows

3.1. Second-order equivalence classes
Equation (5) establishes a universal covariance form: regardless of the specific radial profile U(r), the
entire second-order structure of any cylindrically symmetric OAM eigenmode is captured by just three
quantities, ⟨r2⟩, ⟨k2
r⟩, and ℓ, as derived in appendix A. This motivates defining a second-order equiv-
alence relation: two beams ψ1 and ψ2 are second-order equivalent, written ψ1 ∼ψ2, if and only if
C1 = C2. Under symplectic (ABCD) propagation, the covariance matrix transforms as C 7→SCST, so the
equivalence relation is preserved at every propagation plane: if ψ1 ∼ψ2 at z = 0, then ψ1 ∼ψ2 for all
z. Consequently, all second-order observables, that is the beam width, far-field divergence, M2 factor,
and OAM content, are identical for any two members of the same equivalence class, independently of
the detailed radial structure. The specific beam families considered below (LG, PVB, BG) represent dis-
tinct equivalence classes, each parametrized by their respective values of (⟨r2⟩,⟨k2
r⟩,ℓ). The TGSM beam
matched to each class is identified by the correspondence of equations (10) and (11). We stress that this
is strictly a second-order (covariance-matrix) equivalence, and not a complete physical equivalence: two
fields sharing the same covariance matrix agree on all observables determined by second-order moments,
but may differ in their higher-order statistics, mutual coherence functions, modal decompositions, and
detailed field distributions. In particular, the matched TGSM beam is partially coherent while the OAM
eigenmode is fully coherent, so the two fields remain physically distinct despite sharing the same covari-
ance matrix.

3.6. Parameter determination from the covariance matrix within a mode family
Although a single covariance matrix may represent both a TGSM beam and a coherent OAM beam, as
shown above, it uniquely determines the TGSM beam through the parameters σ, u, and δ [14]. Thus,
two distinct TGSM beams cannot share the same CM. The LG, PVB and BG beams are non-gaussian
functions, in general, and thus it is to be expected that they cannot be determined by the elements of
the CM alone. However, somewhat surprisingly, we note here that the CM is indeed sufficient in some
cases. First, for all beams of the form (1), the parameter ℓfollows directly from the anti-diagonal ele-
ments of equation (5), so that only the remaining parameters need to be assessed: w0 and p for LG
modes, w and R for PVBs, and wb and kb for BG beams.
For an LG beam, the radial parameter p can be determined from the product ⟨x2⟩⟨k2
x⟩, as shown in
equation (19). Since ℓis already fixed and p⩾0, this relation specifies p unambiguously. Substituting this
value into equation (15) or equation (16) then determines w0. Therefore, the covariance matrix uniquely
determines the LG mode parameters.
For PVB and BG beams, the diagonal elements of the covariance matrix provide two equations for
the two beam parameters. Although these equations are nonlinear, we can verify that they admit no
additional solutions within the regime of validity of the asymptotic moments.

4. Beam propagation and M2 factor equivalence
A direct consequence of the CM equivalence is that the full TGSM propagation toolbox applies to any
matched coherent OAM beam. Once the parameter identifications of section 3 are made, all TGSM
results for beam-width evolution, Rayleigh range, and far-field divergence under arbitrary ABCD sys-
tems carry over without further calculation. For a beam propagating in free space, the covariance matrix
evolves as C(z) = S(z)C(0)S(z)T, where S(z) is the symplectic transfer matrix for free-space propagation
a distance z [47]. Because two beams with the same C(0) are mapped by the same S(z), they have the
same C(z) at every plane. In particular, they share the same beam-width evolution, the same Rayleigh
range, and the same far-field divergence angle. We emphasize, however, that this propagation equiv-
alence is restricted to observables that are fully determined by the second-order moments and evolve
under symplectic (ABCD) transformations. Quantities that depend on the phase-singularity structure,
the detailed modal content, higher-order coherence functions, or other non-Gaussian features of the field
are not captured by the covariance matrix, and therefore cannot be inferred from the correspondence.
At the reference plane z = 0, chosen such that the position–momentum correlation vanishes, a com-
pact measure of this propagation equivalence is the beam-quality parameter

5. Conclusion
In this work we established a correspondence between the covariance matrices of TGSM beams and
those of coherent OAM-carrying beams, including LG, BG, and PVBs, showing that these matrices share
the same structural form. As a consequence, the covariance matrix alone may not uniquely identify the
family to which an OAM beam belongs. More broadly, equation (5) defines a second-order equivalence
relation on cylindrically symmetric OAM beams: two such beams are second-order equivalent if and
only if they share the same values of ⟨r2⟩, ⟨k2
r⟩, and ℓ, and this equivalence is preserved under all sym-
plectic propagation. The coherent beam families studied here and the matched TGSM beams belong
to the same equivalence classes in this sense. In particular, within the regimes considered here, a sin-
gle covariance matrix can represent BG and PVBs, as well as high-order LG modes. We also examined
the conditions under which the same covariance matrix can describe both a TGSM beam and a coher-
ent OAM beam. Because two beams sharing a covariance matrix are mapped identically by any sym-
plectic (ABCD) transformation, they exhibit the same beam-width evolution, Rayleigh range, far-field
divergence, and M2 beam-quality factor. This expands the experimental flexibility in situations where
only the beam’s OAM and spreading are relevant, and the detailed radial structure of the field is not
important. Conversely, the correspondence provides a prescription for designing a partially coherent
TGSM beam that mimics the propagation and OAM content of any specified coherent OAM eigen-
state, without reproducing its full field structure. It is important to emphasize that the correspondence
is restricted to second-order statistics: it does not imply equality of higher-order statistics, coherence
functions, modal content, or the detailed field distributions. We further examined the invertibility of
the covariance matrix within each beam family. Surprisingly, for LG modes, the beam parameters are
uniquely and exactly determined by the second-order moments. For PVB and BG beams, the CM deter-
mines the beam parameters to leading order in the relevant approximation regime, though a proof of

exact uniqueness from the full moment equations remains an open question. We also showed in the
appendix that the PVB modes span the full space of transverse fields for every ring width w > 0, so that
any field can be expanded in them. At finite w the family is non-orthogonal and redundant, and the
expansion returns the field low-pass filtered on the scale w; only in the narrow-ring limit w →0 do the
modes become a genuine orthonormal (delta-normalized) basis with exact reconstruction. The practi-
cal condition for the PVB expansion to faithfully represent a given field is w ≪λmin, where λmin is the
shortest transverse length scale of the field, with the OAM spectrum being reproduced exactly for any
w. Finally, we note that structured light fields such as those studied here are finding a growing range of
applications, for example in optical communications [48], superoscillation phenomena [49], quasilattice
vortex fields [50], and vector-field mapping [51]. In such contexts, where the robustness of second-order
observables under propagation is often the relevant figure of merit, the second-order equivalence estab-
lished here, which allows the propagation and OAM content of a coherent OAM beam to be reproduced
by a partially coherent TGSM beam, may prove particularly useful.
